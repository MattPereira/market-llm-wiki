# Safety First

**Arthur Hayes** · 2026-09-21 · [read](https://cryptohayes.substack.com/p/safety-first)

**The safety explanation.** Hayes doubts that Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceX would slow AGI development mainly out of concern for humanity. He suspects their economics instead: customers want AI at what he calls the China price, roughly one-hundredth the US price, while Chinese model quality is catching up. In his telling, US firms answer that competition by invoking safety and asking the government to regulate and fund the race with China. He questions their revenue growth and the cost of serving customers, but says their financial disclosures have yet to give him answers.

**Compute demand and debt.** He says the three firms make no profit, yet their expected purchases of training and inference compute underpin more than $1 trillion of investment-grade debt and hundreds of billions in riskier debt and loans. Profitable technology companies help backstop financing for data centers and chips. If labs slow frontier training and make inference more efficient, compute demand falls while the debt used to build capacity remains. Its price could decline well before defaults. For Hayes, the crucial question is who holds that debt with leverage. He points to insurance policyholders, via private-equity-owned insurers, and poses two possible government responses: buy the unused compute for national-security reasons or rescue insurers whose AI debt loses value. He expects either route to increase dollar liquidity and lift Bitcoin.

## In the name of Choyna

**A national-security buyer.** Hayes argues that a contest with China could persuade the US government to spend on frontier AI despite weak private-market demand. He objects to using public money to override that price signal. Under his first scenario, the government signs offtake agreements that guarantee labs revenue, uses the compute to develop AGI, owns the resulting models, and could lease them back to labs for commercial inference. Government ownership would also give it control over who could use the models, unlike private labs selling globally. He doubts that this could keep knowledge from China for long, citing Chinese progress despite chip restrictions and the Soviet acquisition of atomic-bomb knowledge.

**Paying for it.** The government would borrow to fund the purchases. Hayes says Treasury Secretary Bessent and Fed Chair Warsh see AI as sufficiently productive to justify more debt. He cites June 2026 nominal GDP growth of 6.6% against an effective federal-funds rate near 3.6%. At those rates, he sees roughly a three-point advantage for short-term borrowing, though debt relative to GDP falls only if the deficit is no more than 3%. Much of that growth, in his account, comes from the data-center buildout itself, which depends on labs buying compute.

**The Fed and the banks.** Hayes says the Fed unanimously raised its policy rate by 0.25 percentage points for the first time since July 2023, and stopped its RMP Treasury-bill purchases on August 14. Without cheaper or more plentiful Fed money, extra issuance could raise mortgage, car-loan, and credit-card rates, deepening opposition to AI construction. He thinks Trump and Bessent would need at least seven FOMC members to change course to make this route easier. Yet he argues that commercial banks can create money while the Fed tightens: since August 14, he says, their assets have grown by more than $100 billion amid looser liquidity constraints. The rate hike also adds an estimated $7.5 billion a year in interest on banks’ excess reserves at the Fed. He expects that income to support lending and speculation. Combining bank expansion with Fed policy, he calls the net effect stimulative enough to accommodate government borrowing for compute.

## Captive Insurance

**Where the losses could land.** If the government does not buy the compute, Hayes expects the debt that financed it to lose value. Drawing on **[Nick Nemeth’s account of captive reinsurance](https://mispricedassets.substack.com/p/the-hole?utm_source=publication-search)**, he traces a possible route from that loss to an insurance bailout. After 2008, private-equity firms could borrow cheaply to buy businesses with steady cash flow, add debt, cut costs, and extract dividends. As purchase prices and later borrowing costs rose, returns fell. Hayes says some firms bought life insurers and annuity providers, installed themselves as investment managers, and put their own private-equity and private-credit assets into portfolios backed by policyholders’ premiums. Those premiums are attractive because payouts often lie decades ahead.

**The capital backstop.** Insurers must hold capital against investment losses. An independent reinsurer would price and fund the risk it takes from an insurer. In Hayes’s account, a private-equity-owned insurer can instead rely on an affiliated captive reinsurer with little equity, reporting an apparent risk-sharing asset without an equally strong source of cash. He says some states, including Vermont, allow details of these arrangements to be sealed, making the true buffer hard to assess.

**A fictional failure.** To illustrate, Hayes imagines a life insurer buying $10 billion of highly rated debt from a failing crypto company and reinsuring it through an affiliate capitalized with just $1 for each $100 of covered assets. The insurer treats the debt as protected. Once the borrower misses a payment and the debt is downgraded to junk, regulators require more capital, but the affiliate cannot supply it. The insurer’s shortfall becomes visible. He says state guarantees generally cover only $250,000–$300,000 of a policyholder’s claim, and surviving insurers fund the guarantees after failures; he contrasts that with banks’ advance payments for FDIC protection.

**From the example to AI credit.** Hayes substitutes private credit to software companies threatened by AI and debt issued to finance data centers for the fictional crypto debt. The latter depends on large labs consuming enough compute to repay it. He points to major private-equity firms’ participation in data-center financing and software lending, while saying opaque captive-insurer books leave their actual exposures and asset quality unknown. Citing Nemeth, he gives $1.54 trillion as the estimated affiliated-reinsurance asset, without claiming to know its real value. As an illustration, he cites a Brookfield reinsurance asset booked at $1.48 billion although the protection provider reportedly told a regulator it owed nothing.

**The bailout trigger.** Hayes expects a shortfall in compute sales to prompt ratings agencies to downgrade investment-grade, securitized data-center debt. Insurers holding the senior tranches would need fresh capital, and their captive reinsurers might be unable to provide it. He expects officials to prevent large insurer failures, comparing the situation with the 2008 AIG bailout. In his second scenario, support arrives in smaller increments to keep an insurance crisis from becoming visible.

## More of the Same

Hayes does not expect an immediate surge in money creation. He expects either government purchases of compute or support for impaired insurers to expand the supply of dollars, end the crypto market’s choppy stretch, and raise Bitcoin and selected altcoins. As a founder of an AI-and-crypto project that needs cheap compute, he also expects continued data-center construction to create a glut of spot capacity, lower compute costs, and encourage more AI-agent use.
