# Safety First

**Arthur Hayes** · 2026-09-21 · [read](https://cryptohayes.substack.com/p/safety-first)

Hayes doubts that Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceX are slowing AGI development primarily for safety. He reads their “Safety First” position as a response to weak economics: customers want AI, but at what he calls the China price, roughly one-hundredth of the US price. As Chinese models narrow the quality gap, he expects US firms to seek government regulation and funding under a national-security argument. He says the three labs make no profit, yet their expected compute purchases support more than $1 trillion of investment-grade debt and hundreds of billions in riskier debt and loans. Profitable technology companies help backstop the leases, chips, and data centers that depend on those purchases.

Slower frontier-model development would reduce training demand, while a greater focus on efficiency would make each customer request use less compute. Hayes calls this compute demand destruction. The debt that funded capacity remains even if the labs buy less of it, so its market price should fall before defaults arrive. He sees leveraged holders of that debt, especially insurers connected to private equity, as the vulnerable parties. His central question is whether the US government will become the compute buyer of last resort or print money to support insurers holding impaired AI debt. He expects either response to lift Bitcoin and crypto through greater dollar liquidity.

## In the name of Choyna

Hayes expects the government to justify direct support for US AI as a contest with China, despite what he sees as market signals that frontier-model development is uneconomic at current prices. He objects to using taxes and debt to override those signals and doubts that state control of advanced models could keep the underlying knowledge from China for long.

Under his first scenario, the government signs offtake agreements that guarantee AI labs revenue from compute. It uses the capacity to develop frontier models, owns those models, and could lease them back to labs for commercial inference. This would keep data centers in use and give the government greater control over access than it has when private labs sell models globally. Hayes doubts that such control would stop information spreading, citing the failure of semiconductor restrictions to halt Chinese chip development and the Soviet acquisition of atomic-bomb knowledge.

The government would borrow to fund these purchases. Hayes argues that Treasury Secretary Bessent and Fed Chair Warsh view AI as productive enough to support that debt. He cites June 2026 nominal GDP growth of 6.6% against an effective fed funds rate near 3.6%: at those rates, he says, short-term issuance leaves roughly a three-point growth advantage, though debt-to-GDP would fall only if the deficit stays at or below 3%. He attributes much of that growth to the data-center buildout, which itself depends on lab demand for compute.

Fed policy complicates the plan. Hayes says the Fed raised its policy rate by 0.25 percentage points at its latest meeting, its first increase since July 2023, and stopped RMP Treasury-bill purchases on August 14. Without cheaper or more plentiful Fed money, extra government borrowing could raise rates for mortgages, cars, and credit cards, worsening public opposition to AI construction. He says Trump and Bessent would need at least seven FOMC members to change course for that route to become easier.

He nevertheless argues that commercial banks can supply liquidity even while the Fed tightens. Since RMP purchases stopped, he says, banks expanded their assets by more than $100 billion as liquidity rules eased. He also estimates that the rate hike will pay banks another $7.5 billion annually on excess reserves held at the Fed, supporting further lending and speculation. On his combined reading of Fed and bank balance sheets, the net effect is stimulative enough to help finance government compute purchases.

## Captive Insurance

If the government does not buy the compute, Hayes expects AI debt to lose value. Drawing on Nick Nemeth’s account of captive reinsurance, he explains why he thinks insurers could turn that loss into a broader financial problem.

After 2008, cheap borrowing let private-equity firms buy cash-generating businesses, add debt, cut costs, and extract dividends. Higher purchase prices and, later, higher borrowing costs reduced returns. Hayes says some firms then bought life insurers and annuity providers, whose premiums provide long-lived capital, and installed themselves as investment managers. That gave them a way to place their own private-equity and private-credit assets in portfolios ultimately backing policyholders’ claims.

Insurers must hold capital against investment losses. In Hayes’s account, an insurer can transfer apparent risk to an affiliated captive reinsurer that contributes little equity, rather than to an independent reinsurer that would price and fund the risk at arm’s length. He says some jurisdictions permit the arrangement’s details to remain sealed. The parent can then report reinsurance protection whose ability to pay is uncertain.

His fictional Luna–Alameda Insurance example shows the failure path. An insurer buys highly rated debt with policyholder premiums and relies on an affiliated reinsurer with only $1 of equity for each $100 of reinsured assets. If the debt misses payment and is downgraded, the insurer must supply more capital; the affiliate cannot provide enough cash, exposing the shortfall. Hayes says state guarantees generally cover only $250,000–$300,000 of a policyholder’s claim. He argues that surviving insurers fund those guarantees after failures, unlike banks’ prefunded FDIC insurance.

He then substitutes real exposures for the example’s fictional debt: private credit to software companies under pressure from AI and debt funding data centers whose repayment depends on the largest labs buying compute. He points to private-equity firms’ participation in data-center financing and says opaque captive-insurer books make the quality of their holdings hard to assess. Citing Nemeth, he puts the estimated affiliated-reinsurance asset at $1.54 trillion, while acknowledging that its real value is unknown. As one illustration, he cites a Brookfield reinsurance asset booked at $1.48 billion even though the entity providing the protection reportedly told a regulator it owed nothing.

The trigger he anticipates is a downgrade of investment-grade, securitized AI data-center debt after compute sales fall short. An insurer would need fresh capital against the downgraded assets, but its captive reinsurer might not have the cash. Hayes expects officials to prevent large insurer failures, drawing an analogy to the 2008 AIG bailout. In this second scenario, he expects money creation to arrive incrementally as authorities try to avoid a visible insurance crisis.

## More of the Same

Hayes does not expect an immediate surge in money creation. He sees a choice between government-backed compute demand and support for insurers after AI credit losses; either way, he expects dollar supply to grow, the choppy crypto market to turn upward, and Bitcoin and selected altcoins to benefit. As a founder of an AI and crypto project that needs inexpensive compute, he also expects continued data-center construction to create excess capacity and cheaper spot compute, encouraging more AI-agent use.
