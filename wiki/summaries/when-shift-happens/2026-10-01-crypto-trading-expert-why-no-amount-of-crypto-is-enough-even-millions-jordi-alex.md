# Crypto Trading Expert: Why No Amount of Crypto Is Enough (even millions) | Jordi Alexander | E188

**When Shift Happens** · 2026-10-01 · 1:53:48 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uQonACNyTWM)

## Success means sustaining growth

Jordi Alexander, founder of Selini Capital, says he has achieved most of his lifelong goals, including access to influential people across industries. The reward is learning how exceptional people think. Wealth or fame alone does not establish talent: he asks who would succeed across many different versions of the world, rather than through fortunate circumstances.

Experience has reduced his imposter syndrome and made him more willing to act decisively. But the intense multitasking that helped him build his firm was exhausting. He now accepts missing smaller opportunities to preserve attention for major trends. His health analogy is similar: reaching a body-fat target briefly means little; maintaining an improved average over months requires lasting habits and recovery after disruptions.

## AI rewards judgment beyond execution

**Uneven intelligence.** Alexander rejects straightforward extrapolations from model progress to imminent AGI. AI excels in some categories and stalls in others. The economically relevant question is whether people are being replaced, and he does not see broad replacement as close, although some jobs and skills are already disappearing. He expects a divide between people who use new technology effectively and those who cannot or will not adapt, extending beyond wealth to capabilities and health.

He also rejects simple solutions such as buying Bitcoin and forgetting everything else. Quantum risk is one possible failure case. Complex financial and health systems require models with multiple interacting factors; AI can help organize them. His own bond-trading intuition once failed when an oil spike pushed yields beyond his expectations. He had omitted an unusual but consequential driver of inflation and interest rates. The lesson is to keep updating mental models and retain uncertainty.

**Builders have a window.** Proactive people deploying armies of agents currently enjoy an exceptional advantage. Over the next decade, however, he expects labs to automate more of the orchestration itself. Pure execution could become less distinctive. Thinkers also benefit from rapid access to answers he characterizes as roughly 90th-percentile expertise, but genuine specialists can still see important omissions.

The durable skill is **judgment, or taste**: selecting and refining good outputs, bringing local knowledge, and understanding the audience. Memorized facts and mental arithmetic are increasingly commoditized. He advocates combining first-principles reasoning with empirical feedback rather than accepting whatever an AI proposes. Creativity, vision, and philosophy become more valuable as production gets easier.

## A fixed retirement number misses a moving world

Alexander sees performance as alternating focused execution with periods that restore perspective. He favors activities serving several purposes at once, such as walking outdoors with a friend, rather than staying permanently in an exhausting state of vigilance.

He rejects the idea that $10 million, $20 million, or $30 million guarantees a permanent escape from adaptation. Calculations built on a fixed bond yield and spending budget overlook changing purchasing power, lifestyles, and opportunities. He also believes remaining productive helps people stay mentally engaged. Holidays are compatible with this view; an imagined point at which learning and participation can stop is not.

He predicts enormous wealth creation over the next five to ten years, allowing capable newcomers to catch up while inherited wealth loses relative importance unless allocated well. He cites Middle Eastern investment of oil wealth into AI as an example of following productivity into new assets.

The host challenges whether this framework helps people barely able to save, especially if AI makes necessities cheaper. Alexander turns to status competition and entertainment rather than resolving the affordability question directly. He controversially estimates that only 10–15% of people can meaningfully influence developments even if they push themselves; he rejects the claim that everyone has an exceptional contribution waiting to emerge.

He evaluates health interventions through perceived risks and benefits rather than rejecting every shortcut. He distinguishes improved functioning from appearance alone and reports personal benefits from experimental interventions. These are his experiences and judgments; he acknowledges he is not a doctor. Valuing limited time also leads him to abandon small recoveries of money when the administrative effort exceeds their value to him.

## Bitcoin optimism, gambling skepticism, and valuation discipline

**Bitcoin.** Alexander remains invested and calls it undervalued, with high expectations for the following twelve months. His reasoning is that equities' recent pause and growing discussion of bond intervention and dollar devaluation could redirect attention toward gold and Bitcoin. He regards Bitcoin as a better version of gold but sees quantum threats and the absence of a dedicated coordinating team as tail risks.

He notes the strong performance of the S&P 500 and Nasdaq since 2008 and believes governments have incentives to sustain widely held assets. But no single allocation rule covers every future scenario. He wants to focus increasingly on capital allocation because directing resources productively matters both to individual wealth and civilization.

**Speculation is not a general escape route.** Sports betting and crypto trading offer an illusion of control; few participants understand probabilities well enough to possess an edge. He wishes society gambled less but will still participate professionally in those markets. Investing in infrastructure can be attractive, depending on the platform and entry valuation.

That distinction shapes his view of Hyperliquid. He praises Jeff's execution and product, yet questions token value at a reported **$80–90 billion fully diluted valuation against less than $1 billion in revenue**. A strong platform does not automatically offer exciting returns at that price. He considers newer platforms such as FOMO harder to assess.

## Zcash as volatile insurance, not assured monetary adoption

Alexander compares Zcash to Bitcoin around 2015–16: long-term success remains uncertain, but speculation can overshoot sharply in both directions. High volatility and trading volume give it utility as a trading asset even before its monetary role is settled. He frames ZEC as insurance against Bitcoin encountering quantum or other problems. If Bitcoin adapts successfully, Zcash might remain a smaller alternative rather than become essential.

He bought around **$300–400**, partly because prominent holders such as Naval and Balaji appeared motivated by philosophy rather than a quick threefold gain. Patient holders allow appreciation without immediate profit taking. A stronger technology focus could also help Zcash adapt, although it reduces the reassurance of long-established stability. He mentions an AI-discovered security problem and expects some profit-taking resistance after the rally.

Without earnings, ZEC cannot be valued through a price-to-earnings ratio; liquidity, flows, and mindshare drive the trade. In the host's one-to-two-year framing, Alexander says **$5,000–10,000 ZEC is possible if Bitcoin reaches $200,000**. Bitcoin failing could create an even stronger insurance payoff. The opposite scenario is stagnation, disappointed holders, and gradual capitulation. The upside is conditional, not a guaranteed target.

For his broader portfolio, he revisits which assets benefit from new models and agent capabilities every week or month. Buying anything with “AI” in its name is not a substitute for tracing how innovation changes its prospects.

## Convert wealth into useful experiences and lasting stability

Alexander separates allocation, which preserves participation in productivity gains, from spending, which converts wealth into personal benefit. He encourages successful people to spend on health, expert instruction, and meaningful experiences with family and friends. He suggests spending at least 10% of income rather than treating endless saving as inherently wise.

Generosity can improve others' lives, but recurring support can create dependence and unappreciated expectations. He adapts employee compensation to personality: some welcome variable upside, while others need predictable increases. Status purchases and relationships that undermine health or stability can similarly turn financial success into unhappiness.

He expects scarcity and money to persist even if intelligence becomes abundant. His concern is failing to use capital strategically or missing valuable experiences, rather than insufficient cost cutting.

Physical and mental health may become deeper divides than wealth. Drawing on his own earlier breakdown, he emphasizes interrupting downward spirals, supportive parenting, and rebuilding confidence. He hopes AI mental-health tools and research into psychedelic therapies can help; he has not personally undergone the latter treatment discussed. His closing principle returns to the opening: reaching a wealth or health target briefly matters less than maintaining an ongoing process of learning and adaptation.
