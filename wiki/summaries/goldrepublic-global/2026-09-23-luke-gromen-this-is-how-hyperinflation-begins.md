# Luke Gromen: This Is How Hyperinflation Begins

**GoldRepublic Global** · 2026-09-23 · 1:25:27 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9dUte6c7ZcM)

## Either Fed move feeds the fiscal problem

Luke Gromen argues that the Federal Reserve is trapped by US debt. Cutting rates while inflation exceeds target could weaken the dollar. Raising them initially restrains private spending, but also raises interest payments on roughly **$40 trillion of debt**, much of it held domestically. He treats that interest as income for bondholders and says higher refinancing costs eventually swell deficits. In his figures, interest plus entitlements and veterans' benefits already consume **105% of federal receipts** through the fiscal third quarter, while those obligations grow faster than receipts. A rate hike could slow inflation for a few months, he says, before making it worse as debt reprices over the following 12–14 months. He acknowledges debate over whether the private-sector slowdown outweighs the added interest income.

He says this constraint reflects decades of wars, bailouts, and pandemic spending, not a choice the Fed can now avoid. Because government outlays are nearly a quarter of GDP in his account, and transfers supply a large share of household income, he doubts that slowing private activity alone can restore fiscal balance. A stronger dollar after a hike could also force foreign Treasury holders to sell bonds to defend their own currencies, raising long-term yields even as the Fed tries to restrain demand.

**What could change his view.** Gromen says sustained disinflation would require the Fed and Treasury to tolerate failed Treasury auctions and much higher market rates, or lawmakers to cut entitlements; he expects neither. He also names a return to large foreign purchases of low-real-yield Treasuries, especially by China or Russia, or a major change in older Americans' life expectancy that slows benefit growth. These are conditions under which his thesis could fail, though he considers them unlikely.

## Bonds, the dollar, and the next recession

Gromen says the dollar, long-term Treasuries, and entitlements cannot all preserve their real value. Cutting benefits would depress spending by older households and might enlarge the deficit through weaker growth. He therefore expects **Treasuries to pay their nominal claims but lose purchasing power**, with negative real rates reducing the debt burden. He invokes a historical study of highly indebted sovereigns as support, including Japan's recent bond losses, while presenting its record as his argument rather than proof of the US outcome. He expects the dollar to weaken particularly against creditor currencies such as the yuan and eventually the yen; he is less confident against the euro and expects the pound to do worse. Because currency pairs are hard to time, he prefers gold as a neutral reserve asset in a system where trade is increasingly settled across currencies and, in his view, ultimately in gold.

In that prospective system, he ranks the yuan and yen as losing the least against gold, followed by the euro, dollar, and pound. The relative order matters to him more than a blanket call for a weaker dollar against every currency. He expects China to make gold settlement accessible to holders of offshore yuan, reinforcing his preference for gold over an attempt to trade the yuan directly.

**Why yields rise.** The host asks why US Treasury term premiums have risen while China has faced deflation. Gromen says cheaper Chinese goods should ease US import inflation if trade remains dollar-centered. He reads the divergence between Chinese bond yields and US term premiums as evidence of inflation concern, insufficient Treasury demand, or movement away from dollar settlement. He then argues that higher US rates can create their own bond supply: a stronger dollar pressures countries with dollar debts to sell Treasuries for cash, while recession or market volatility pushes leveraged hedge funds, banks, and pensions to sell their holdings. He cites about **$9.4 trillion** in foreign-held Treasuries and **$13–14 trillion** in offshore dollar debt as exposures behind that risk.

Starting with a roughly $2.1 trillion annual federal deficit, Gromen sketches possible foreign sales, hedge-fund deleveraging, pension sales, and recession-related fiscal deterioration. These are scenarios, not observed additions to the deficit: combined, he says, they could leave the market needing to absorb $5.8–7 trillion in effective Treasury supply. He therefore predicts that in the next recession **long-term yields will rise after a brief dip**, even while stocks fall. The stress threshold depends on the dollar and oil price as well as the ten-year yield: he regards a 5% yield with expensive oil and a strong dollar as much more dangerous than the same yield with cheaper oil and a weaker dollar.

**The policy response.** He expects Treasury buybacks and other measures called debt management to expand as yields rise. He doubts officials will openly announce yield-curve control because a formal cap could oblige them to buy Treasuries, mortgages, and other dollar debt indefinitely. The economic effect could still resemble it. He cannot identify the tipping point and expects greater volatility in bonds, currencies, gold, and Bitcoin; accordingly, he wants exposure to his thesis without leverage.

He gives a historical caution for that sizing choice: someone using leverage to own gold during the German mark's collapse could have been liquidated repeatedly, despite getting the ultimate currency direction right. For Gromen, surviving the interim price swings matters more than forecasting the precise date of a monetary break.

## AI and energy tighten the squeeze

Gromen calls the next pressure an **AI-driven bond singularity**: AI companies compete with Treasury for financing before their promised productivity benefits can rescue public finances. Citing an industry estimate, he says AI capital spending from 2024 through 2029 could total $11 trillion, with about $5 trillion borrowed. At the same time, he expects AI to displace well-paid administrative work, especially in healthcare, cutting employment-tax receipts and weakening households' ability to pay mortgages and consumer debt. He argues that this falling tax base and growing borrowing need could force ever-larger monetary support well before an AI intelligence singularity. The job-loss and financing paths are his forecasts, not established results.

**Physical scarcity changes behavior.** An oil shock, he says, gives foreign holders another reason to sell Treasuries: energy and food take priority over financial reserves. He is especially concerned about disrupted diesel and sulfuric-acid supplies, which could constrain metals production just as AI and power infrastructure need more copper and silver. An executive at an electrical-equipment company told him he would rather hold copper in a warehouse than cash in a bank because future supply is uncertain. Gromen sees that preference spreading through industrial supply chains as the early psychology of hyperinflation: firms stockpile usable inputs when they doubt money will buy them later. He also warns that scarce grid components could make outages slow to repair. This is his interpretation of a reported conversation and potential shortages, rather than evidence that hyperinflation has begun.

## From reserve sales to the debasement trade

Gold can initially fall during an energy shock because holders sell reserves to pay for oil, Gromen says. Over time he expects scarce physical goods to expose the weak real value of sovereign and private debt. Then investors seeking to leave a much larger bond market could drive gold sharply higher; he thinks Bitcoin might signal that turn early. He puts a possible shift in market perception **months, rather than years, away**, while allowing that a diplomatic or trade agreement could delay it. He does not claim to know the exact trigger.

Gromen says the dollar-debasement trade favors **accumulating gold, Bitcoin, and silver**. He had sold most of his Bitcoin around $95,000–$96,000, expecting a deeper decline, but is turning more constructive as the energy and debt stresses persist. He advises ordinary holders against trying to trade every move, while stressing position size: Bitcoin once reached nearly half his liquid net worth, more volatility than he wants at his age. He regards Bitcoin as the hardest asset but prefers a larger gold allocation for its longer record and lower volatility; silver also benefits, in his view, from industrial scarcity.

He remains bullish on gold miners and owns them, expecting gold ultimately to reach at least five figures in dollars. Yet higher diesel costs or outright fuel rationing could squeeze or halt mines. He also considers government claims on exceptional mining profits, or nationalization, a new tail risk if countries demand physical gold for strategic goods. Consequently, he expects **bullion to outperform miners** over the full cycle and uses bullion as his main expression of the thesis.

Finally, Gromen speculates that unusual US nonmonetary-gold exports through the UK and Switzerland toward China may represent payment for Chinese rare earths. He explicitly says he has no direct confirmation: the gold flows and US concern over rare earths are the clues behind his theory, not evidence of an agreed gold-for-materials trade.
