# The Shrugflation Triangle: How AI, Oil, and Treasuries are Impacting the Economy

**Kyla Scanlon** · 2026-10-14 · [read](https://kyla.substack.com/p/the-shrugflation-triangle-how-ai)

## Oil, AI, and Treasuries reinforce one another

Scanlon calls the economy's central feedback loop **shrugflation**: an oil shock raises inflation and interest rates, but its biggest borrowers keep spending. The government must fund its bills regardless of rates; AI companies see investment as necessary for survival. Their financing needs can push long-term yields higher, while data centers require energy that is already expensive. Households absorb higher fuel and borrowing costs while investment gains accrue elsewhere.

## The energy shock squeezes consumers twice

Scanlon attributes expensive fuel to the Iran war's disruption of the Strait of Hormuz and Saudi Arabia's alternative pipeline. Even with some flows restored, shipping constraints remain: she cites supertanker rental costs of about $1.4 million daily against $30,000 before the war, and September diesel prices of $6.53 a gallon, up 77% annually. Consumers pay both for transporting goods and for driving to buy them. A G7 reserve release of 100 million barrels offers only temporary relief.

Higher rates can suppress spending but cannot reopen shipping routes. Expectations compound the problem: Scanlon cites October Michigan consumer sentiment of 46.3 and one-year inflation expectations of 4.7%, alongside a retailer planning price increases beyond its actual cost increases. Retreating from Fed forward guidance risks weakening another tool for anchoring expectations. Oil and Treasury yields moving together transmit the supply shock into financing costs.

## AI's gains and repayment assumptions are uneven

**Ownership determines who benefits.** Scanlon contrasts median net worth of roughly $10,000 for renters with more than $450,000 for homeowners. Drawing on Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick, she says owners captured $15,000 of an $18,000 increase in company value added per worker between 2001 and 2021; workers received $3,000. AI may amplify this distribution even while making some tasks easier. Cheap generation also enables scams and fills shared digital spaces with low-quality material.

She distinguishes these concerns from evidence of mass unemployment: researchers find sweeping displacement forecasts unreliable, and software-engineer postings have risen over two years. The immediate question is who receives the gains from work, rather than whether work disappears.

**Demand must catch up with investment.** Scanlon highlights **[Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh's paper on the AI buildout](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/4c_Van-Nieuwerburgh.pdf)**, which estimates AI revenue must reach about 9% of GDP by 2032 for the investment to pay off. Yet bank-account studies find only 2–3% of households pay for chatbots, and prospective customers outside technology are less optimistic about their own businesses. Rapid revenue growth leaves room for upside, but the required scale remains enormous.

Reported revenue also needs interpretation. Annualized monthly run rates differ from realized annual sales, and gross versus net accounting for cloud-distributed services makes comparisons misleading. Scanlon attributes the difference between OpenAI's circulated $70 billion run rate and a roughly $50 billion investor figure to that distinction. She cites OpenAI's projected cash burn and Anthropic's infrastructure obligations as evidence that growth has not resolved financing needs.

**Pricing power is uncertain.** Jefferies' Chris Wood expects freely available Chinese models to commoditize AI and destroy invested capital; Ramp's Ara Kharazian instead emphasizes American providers undercutting one another. In-house models offer customers another alternative. In either case, falling prices threaten repayment of fixed obligations.

Scanlon cites Carlyle's estimate that over 80% of anticipated AI capital spending through 2032 requires external financing. Lenders initially rely on hyperscalers' established businesses rather than demonstrated AI profitability. The five largest spenders are expected to invest about $800 billion this year, roughly their operating cash generation. She contrasts GPUs' rapid depreciation with durable railroads and fiber: debt can outlive useful hardware, with early chip cohorts aging further in 2027–2029.

## Borrowers compete while other sectors slow

Scanlon places AI financing alongside a federal deficit of about $2 trillion and debt roughly equal to GDP. Governments abroad also face rising financing costs. Higher Treasury yields flow into mortgages, auto loans, and refinancing, hurting borrowers with less freedom to keep spending.

She preserves an important dispute: FT Alphaville finds AI borrowing probably does not yet explain Treasury-market crowding out, emphasizing economic strength and geopolitics. Matt King's alternative emphasizes borrowers' determination to issue debt against investors' expanding choices, rather than current issuance volume alone.

Competition for physical resources is clearer. Scanlon cites August data-center construction rising 73% annually while factory construction fell about 20% and homebuilding about 5%. Data centers bid for electrical components used elsewhere, spreading inflation pressure. Apollo's Torsten Slok calls this Dutch disease: a relatively rate-insensitive boom draws capital, power, and labor away from sensitive sectors. Expanding chip and power supply could relieve constraints, but higher rates initially restrain everyone around the boom.

## Credit, power, and equity markets expose limits

**Cloud backlogs depend on lab customers.** Scanlon treats Oracle's rising debt-insurance costs and proximity to junk status as warning signs. A downgrade or trouble at OpenAI could trigger bond selling and spread through interconnected suppliers. Cloud-company contracted backlogs exceed $2 trillion, but much depends on AI labs whose customers can reduce spending. A contractual obligation is not cash already collected.

Financing increasingly reaches insurers and pension funds, while Nvidia explores insurance against customer defaults and inadequate chip resale values. Scanlon argues this redistributes risk into annuities and retirement savings rather than eliminating it. Insurers' declining appetite for long-dated AI bonds suggests limits to available funding.

**The grid may bind before financing does.** Local opposition has canceled projects, while developers' duplicate utility requests inflate apparent demand. Utilities seek binding commitments so abandoned projects do not leave other customers paying for unused capacity. Scanlon's clearest illustration is Oracle trucking natural gas to data centers at roughly four times pipeline-gas costs while projects await power and permits: diesel, AI, and debt meet directly.

AI investment also supports a narrow equity boom. Scanlon cites almost half of stock-market earnings growth coming from AI-related investment, even as many individual stocks and businesses weaken. One company's capital spending becomes another's revenue. If spending slows while debts remain and new shares reach the market, falling stock prices could reduce household wealth and transmit the reversal beyond technology.

## Governance and human welfare are the missing objectives

Scanlon argues dependence on AI-led growth discourages US regulation. She rejects the assumption that competition with China requires abandoning rules: China's extensive controls coexist with frontier progress, although its system concentrates power in the state. She supports governance blueprints addressing who benefits and who bears costs.

Benefits also depend on application. She cites weak measured productivity gains after accounting for code review and insurers' claims that AI-assisted hospital billing raises costs. Consumer agents could cancel unwanted subscriptions or improve deposit returns, but could also destabilize banks' cheap funding and overwhelm restaurants with automated requests. Overreliance may erode learning and tacit human knowledge.

Her closing position follows the Luddites' objection to ownership and displacement: **technology should improve people's agency, dignity, and lives**. GDP, investment, and stock prices cannot establish that outcome. An economy rewarding a small circle of capital owners while households pay more has failed the human purpose of growth.
