# How to Get Rich in America

**Kyla Scanlon** · 2026-08-13 · [read](https://kyla.substack.com/p/how-to-get-rich-in-america)

Scanlon argues the economy's payoff structure has shifted: owning houses and
stocks makes people rich in a way labor income cannot. That breaks the link
between effort and outcome, and people respond the way she picks up other
people's accents—by pattern-matching the environment they are in.

## Part I. Labor vs Capital

Citing Greg Ip (WSJ): labor's share of gross domestic income fell from about 58%
in 1980 to about 51% late last year, while corporate profits' share rose from 7%
to 11.7%. Ip blames the fall of unions, automation, and superstar firms
employing fewer workers—IBM, the most profitable company in 1985, had 400,000
workers; Nvidia, 20x more valuable inflation-adjusted, employs about a tenth of
that. Paul Krugman points to the tax code: the corporate income tax went from
over a fifth of federal revenue in the 1950s to under a tenth, while the payroll
tax grew from a sliver to the second-largest source. Framed as one company,
workers' cut fell from almost 60 cents to about 50 per dollar while owners' rose
by two-thirds (7 to 12 cents)—a loss of $8,000–$12,000 a year per worker,
depending on the calculation.

### How to make money

Income is either wages or nonwage income (Social Security, retirement
disbursements, capital gains, self-employment, and so on). Per the Minneapolis
Fed, the median American earns about $37k in wages and $3,000 in nonwage income,
and inflation has wiped out wage gains over the past four months. Nonwage income
concentrates among the very rich and the old. John Burn-Murdoch (FT) finds
median household net worth roughly doubled in real terms since the mid-1990s on
stock and home prices, while incomes grew about 50%; in the UK, climbing from
the bottom to the top wealth quartile on an average salary took about 20 years
of saving a generation ago and takes 40 now. For the top 1%, 42% of nonwage
income is capital gains; the second-largest piece is S-corp income, which Fed
researchers call "labor in the economic sense." By Scanlon's math, at least a
quarter of what the richest filers report is pure gains. Wealth begets wealth.

### Passive income

People notice. Over half of Americans, including 60% of Gen Z adults, say a
full-time job won't meet their goals (WSJ); 1 in 4 has a side hustle, and Google
interest in "passive income" is up about 50% in five years. Much of that world
is grifters selling courses that don't work. Work dissatisfaction feeds it: 1 in
4 white-collar workers has gone five years without a promotion or raise, and
people—especially those without a college degree—are leaving the labor force.
Culture reflects the shift: Inkoo Kang (New Yorker) describes "broke TV" such as
*DTF St. Louis*, *Beef*, and *The Comeback* displacing *White Lotus* and
*Succession*. Meanwhile the US added 1,200 millionaires a day in 2025, mostly
from stock gains, and financial assets make up almost 80% of gross US wealth.

### Everything is the AI Trade Now

The AI boom is the newest installment: asset holders get very rich off a
technology that threatens work and raises the price of computers and
electricity. The era's implied lesson, Scanlon says, is that hard work is
meaningless and only money brings meaning. Neil Dutta (Bloomberg) ties
stock-wealth consumption, state data-center revenue, and Caterpillar and Cummins
trading like tech stocks into one "AI wealth effect." Data-center backlash
follows: in June, data-center construction hit almost $70 billion annualized
while outlays on everything else, including homes and hospitals, fell $100
billion.

Zilan Qian (Asterisk) notes over 85% of Chinese respondents see AI as more
beneficial than harmful versus under 45% of Americans, and 73% and 88% of
Chinese trust and use AI at work versus 52% and 48% of Americans—but reads it as
a coping mechanism: change oneself. Americans are adapting too:

- The World Economic Forum guesses AI might displace 92 million jobs by 2030.
- 1 in 3 corporate employers are changing hiring plans because of AI and older
  workers staying longer.
- Stanford researchers find jobseekers need 25 applications to land one
  second-round interview.
- Most job growth is in healthcare, driven by an aging, wealthy older
  population.

Adrian Wooldridge (Bloomberg) says economic revolutions breed grievance by
disrupting established ways, enriching a few, and stripping agency—and this one
is faster: electricity took four decades to reach regular Americans, ChatGPT hit
100 million users within months, and AI spending could reach $2.5 trillion by
end-2026 with heavy borrowing (much of it, Scanlon adds, through
off-balance-sheet leases).

### Windfalls and the yolofolio

Emi Nietfeld (NYT) writes that young people no longer believe traditional work
reliably pays off; the "casino economy" hands out windfalls instead. They still
save—the average Zoomer started at 19 versus 35 for Boomers (Schwab)—but
Northwestern Mutual finds 80% of Gen Z and 75% of millennials drawn to
speculative investments because they feel behind. Scanlon calls this prospect
theory at generational scale: when normal life is out of reach, lottery-like
bets in crypto, meme stocks, options, and NFTs look rational. A chart shared by
Eric Balchunas shows young traders treating sports betting as a financial
strategy and investment vehicle, and gambling and investing now share
apps—Robinhood offers IRAs alongside prediction markets. If the house requires a
windfall, betting is an incentive response, not stupidity. South Korea is more
intense: nearly 110 million active individual trading accounts, about two per
citizen, driven by unaffordable housing and concentrated in SK Hynix and
Samsung; regulators now require trading exercises before access to leveraged
single-stock ETFs.

The same logic drives students to use AI for schoolwork, and AI erodes the
"effort" half of effort-to-outcome further. Young people want to be
entrepreneurs (3 in 4) and influencers (1 in 2), and say $600k a year is needed
to feel financially successful. Axios cites financial nihilism, high costs,
influencers, mismatched expectations, and career-ladder struggles.

### The presentation is perception

Lauren Greenfield has long argued fame and fortune are the new American dream:
with high inequality and low mobility, "fictitious social mobility — bling and
presentation — has replaced real social mobility." Scanlon describes the loop:
influencers display often-rented wealth, viewers feel shame that turns into
nihilism and misaligned expectations, then pay the same influencers for courses
on catching up. A WSJ/NORC poll finds money is the only value that grew in
importance over 25 years, with 45% of both Democrats and Republicans calling it
very important; 17 of MrBeast's last 26 videos put a dollar amount in the title.
Yet an EY study finds young people prize financial independence and stability
over riches—they are trying to buy security. Over 70% of young Americans still
expect hard work to bring success, more than in Germany, Sweden, China, Korea,
and Japan, but almost 60% distrust most people. Under half of Americans say
capitalism works well, and only 35% say the nation offers good jobs and the
American dream. A young Chinese worker tells the FT the same story: effort no
longer matches reward. Because ownership pays and assets concentrate in one
demographic, the comparison becomes generational.

## Part II: Demographics

Scanlon's NYT piece argued the housing and retirement crises are one: high home
prices fund retirement, so prices functionally cannot fall. Many older readers
heard a threat to take their homes; one said they stay for a 2.875% mortgage
rather than appreciation, which Scanlon calls two sides of the same coin. She
does not blame homeowners—Idrees Kahloon (Atlantic) notes homes cost five times
median income versus 3.5 times in 1984, and Boomer luck was engineered by
Washington through 30-year fixed mortgages and tax deductions—but says honesty
is not blame:

- By 2030, 1 in 5 Americans will be 65+ (1 in 20 in 1920); almost half live in
  counties where over-64s outnumber under-15s, versus 5% in 1990.
- Americans 55+ held a little over half of US wealth in 1989 and hold 74% now;
  the under-40 share fell from 11% to 6.6%.
- People 70+ are 12% of the population (7.5% in 1981) but hold 32% of household
  net worth, up from 20% two decades ago; their shares of equities and real
  estate have nearly doubled since 2007.

The American experiment worked for this cohort; the question is whether it still
works. Ip notes many over 65 still struggle, but retirees drive over a quarter
of consumer spending, so the economy bends around them: more healthcare demand
and more pressure on Social Security's shrinking worker base. Over 80% of people
over 60 say they are doing okay (Fed SHED). They also dominate politics: the
typical general-election voter is 52, half of campaign donations come from
people over 66, retirees get about $43k per person from the government versus
about $4,300 for children and young adults (Penn-Wharton), and elderly-program
spending is projected above 11% of GDP within a decade, up from 6.9% in 2007.
Older Americans support raising taxes on the young to protect benefits (Cato),
and they increasingly write the legislation.

Jesús Fernández-Villaverde says an aging society must redistribute from workers
to retirees through two pipes: visible taxes (Social Security) or invisible
capital income, as 401(k)s collect corporate profits via stock returns—so much
of the anger at corporate profits is a worker-retiree conflict in disguise. Ed
Yardeni's "Boomer Spending Machine" adds that Boomers are insulated from, even
helped by, higher rates—"a source of income and the reason that home prices are
rising!"—and detached from the labor market, so spending increasingly rests on
retirement wealth rather than labor income.

## Part III: I'm Okay, They're Not

Over half of Americans say the American dream is out of reach, citing living and
housing costs (CNBC)—yet over 40% say they have achieved it, and over half of
the rest expect to. In the Fed's SHED, 73% say they are doing okay or living
comfortably, but only 26% rate the national economy good or excellent. People
feel worse off from prices, expenses, and debt, and better off from asset gains:
"The downside of American life is quite literally priced in prices, whereas the
upside is priced in through asset appreciation. Things are fine, as long as
stocks go up." David French calls it a "Group 1 economy for a Group 9 nation,"
with affordability as the price of entry to normal life. David Whitman's
"optimism gap" and Kinder and Kiewiet's pocketbook-versus-sociotropic research
show people judge their own finances and the nation's as separate objects. The
share doing okay has risen over the past decade in every age group except the
youngest, which is flat—and the systems governing all this have grown confusing.

## Part IV: Illegibility and Kludgeocracy

There has never been more information, yet no one knows how to act on it. Steven
Teles's "kludgeocracy" describes complexity layered on until no one understands
the system; Annie Lowrey calls the human cost a "time tax"; Rana Foroohar calls
healthcare's version "shadow work," saying 25% of US healthcare spending goes to
such friction. Scanlon's own insurer denied every appointment and double-billed
her for months behind a support bot.

The Fed has added to it. Forward guidance comes in Delphic (forecasting its own
behavior) and Odyssean (committing in advance) forms; Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has
dropped both, declining even to say what would make him raise rates, on the
logic that "markets should play ball, not the referee." Claudia Sahm says that
leaves no one knowing what the Fed watches or plans, and many of his colleagues
disagree. Faced with a legibility problem, the most powerful economic
institution chose more opacity. When systems are opaque, trust evaporates and
control migrates inward, to domains where input still correlates with output:
the trading account, the supplement stack, the personal brand.

## Part V: Voice, Resignation, and Exit

Since 1952 the American National Election Studies has asked whether "people like
me don't have any say about what the government does" and whether respondents
voted. Inspired by Albert Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*—where voice
keeps institutions alive and exit drains them—Scanlon sorts answers into
believing voters (have a say, voted), angry voters (no say, voted anyway),
standby citizens (have a say, didn't vote), and the resigned (no say, didn't
vote). In 1952 over half were believing voters and 19% angry voters; the lines
tied in 1996 and diverged again. In 2020 the angry voter became the typical
respondent at 50.6%, holding at 49.3% in 2024, about 18 points above
believers—yet the resigned box did not grow. (ANES moved mostly online after
2012, but the angry-voter lead holds among 2024 in-person interviews.)

The split is by age. Among Americans 55+, the resigned were only 7.5% in 2024,
and just over 55% are angry voters, the highest of any group. Under-35s, over
60% of whom were believing voters in 1960, withdrew: in 2024 the resigned hit
over 23%, up six points in one cycle, over 60% said they had no say, and
reported voting fell 8 points. But they look lost rather than indifferent. They
pay less attention to politics (Gallup/Kettering) yet are the most news-trusting
cohort at 33% (Reuters Institute); they are more than twice as likely as elders
to report four or more barriers to civic participation, nearly all external; and
they are the most likely to say they wanted to volunteer but didn't. Only 40% of
the youngest adults know how to share a concern with elected officials versus
63% of the oldest; civic education raises that from 40% to almost 70%.

By Hirschman's standard, American democracy runs on its angriest voice ever,
while the young show the highest desire to participate and the worst verdict on
the system they inherit—a map problem. Scanlon concludes that when the economy
rewards ownership over effort, systems become uninspectable, and work no longer
buys a normal life, people don't stop caring; they optimize—chasing speculative
windfalls in the economy and exiting ("gray rock") in politics. That is a
well-calibrated read of incentives, which is hopeful: mood is downstream of the
environment, and the environment can be changed.
