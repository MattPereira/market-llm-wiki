# Arthur Hayes On The Early Days Of BitMEX, Where Bitcoin Is Going, And Why We Are Over-Indexing On AI

**1000x** · 2026-10-14 · 48:48 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=clWKbuJG5-M)

## From Lehman to Bitcoin

Arthur Hayes, recorded at Token 2049 in Singapore, chose to study Chinese and move to Asia after reading a book about an American trader in Tokyo. After a study abroad in Hong Kong, he joined Deutsche Bank there and has spent his entire career, roughly 20 years, in greater China. He rejects nationalism and passports as concepts.

**His first day on the trading desk was the day Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy.** The crisis ended the expectation that bankers would get rich quickly. After Citibank let him go in 2013, he found Bitcoin, began arbitrage trading, and developed the idea for BitMEX.

## Building BitMEX on leverage

Hayes founded BitMEX in 2014 with engineers **Ben Delo** (trading system) and **Samuel Reed** (web architecture). He chose derivatives because spot exchanges already had clear winners, while derivatives barely existed. The main precedent, ICBIT, created standards still used today, such as inverse futures, but nearly failed. When Bitcoin ran from about $250 to $1,300 over four months in 2013, its December future traded 100% above spot. Hayes sold the future and bought spot. When the basis collapsed, ICBIT could not liquidate losing traders effectively, and its accounts did not sum to zero. A large Cumberland trader bailed it out in early 2014.

BitMEX launched a Bitcoin-margined quarterly inverse future in fall 2014 with more conservative margining than its Chinese competitors OKCoin, BTC China, and Huobi. That product failed because traders wanted more leverage and liquidity. BitMEX then adapted Chinese socialized-loss margining and, with better technology, **offered 100x leverage against competitors’ 20x**. That kickstarted trading volume. After Bitcoin rose from about $130 to $600 in 2015, the exchange became cash-flow positive.

## Inventing the perpetual swap

BitMEX invented the perpetual swap in May 2016 to cut support requests. Customers did not understand expiries or why futures traded away from spot, and many emailed accusing the exchange of scamming them. A single product also concentrated liquidity across expiries, helping BitMEX compete with Chinese quarterly futures.

**The first version failed.** It referenced Bitfinex dollar and Bitcoin interest rates, which updated too slowly while crypto rallied, so the perp traded at a massive premium to spot. The key fix was a **self-referencing funding rate**: a premium index and eight-hour lookback that charge traders following the trend and pay market makers to take the other side. Other exchanges have copied the design since.

Hayes says the hard part was engineering, not math. Margining every account asynchronously, tick by tick, required rebuilding the trading engine. BitMEX could do it with KDB, a specialized language used in bank trading systems, but Binance and FTX did not launch perps until around 2020. **He says he had no vision of perps eating the world**. Hyperliquid, Lighter, and now the CME are pursuing them. He calls the CME’s claim that staffing prevents 24/7 trading a joke, given that BitMEX peaked at about 250 employees.

## Shutting down BitMEX

BitMEX shut down a few weeks before the interview. Hayes calls it both sad and happy. Custodying billions of dollars is a terrible way to live, he says, because one mistake can lose billions. He says BitMEX exited **with no customer funds ever lost**. He gave no answer on the remaining **insurance fund of roughly 30,000 BTC**.

On the October 2020 US enforcement action against BitMEX, he says anyone fighting the banking system expects government retaliation and hopes not to become the scapegoat; BitMEX did. He was later **pardoned by Trump**. He says lawyers submitted a petition through the proper channels, with no pardon brokers or cash payments.

## Positioning

Hayes still trades because markets give objective feedback and prices connect every subject. At Maelstrom, he runs a mostly long-only directional book with typical speculative holding periods of one to six months. More than 90% of his net worth is in crypto; the rest is in real estate, gold, stocks, and T-bills.

## “We’re wasting trillions on AI”

Hayes is **not bullish on AI stocks**. He thinks AI will be extremely useful over time but says the world is **wasting trillions of dollars of capital** by paying the wrong price for it today. Market prices should cover a product’s full cost plus profit. None of the frontier labs, Anthropic, OpenAI, or SpaceX, makes money. If they charged enough to cover training and inference, he estimates about **95% of users would stop using AI**.

Cheaper compute does not fix this, he argues. Compute is a commodity whose price keeps falling, while data centers keep getting more expensive because labor, materials, and energy are scarce and data centers compete with consumers for grid power. Yet capital keeps flowing in: SpaceX recently sold about $40 billion of bonds, and Anthropic and OpenAI need roughly **$50–100 billion of debt sales a year**. He cites a 2025 figure of **$3 of cost to generate $1 of revenue**. Comparisons to Amazon and Google in the dot-com bubble are the same story, he says: the technology is useful, but these businesses are poor, and if customers wanted the product at its real cost, the labs would be highly profitable.

## Why the bailout is inevitable

**Hayes puts a 75% probability on the labs being unable to honor their compute commitments without a government bailout.** Demand will not grow enough to pay prices that give the labs positive unit economics at scale. The whole world is in the same trade: the US builds data centers, while North and Southeast Asia supply chips, hardware, and raw materials. If the labs do not consume the trillions of dollars of compute they booked over the next five years, the global economy falters. Politicians who promised that AI productivity would solve 80 years of accumulated debt would look foolish. Voters would ask why $2–3 trillion went to steel and cement instead of healthcare or education. So **AI is too big to fail, and the bailout benefits crypto**. He trades this view as long Bitcoin.

**The social fabric won’t change.** He compares it to 2008, when the US, China, and Europe all bailed out their systems. No politician can win by telling voters that their living standards must fall to pay old debts. Voters instead join the faction that will print money for them. Obama promised to hold bankers accountable but declared them **too big to jail**, expanded the bailouts, and foreclosed on homeowners. Faced with a 30–40% economic contraction or another eight years of can-kicking, every leader prints. China is the same: the Communist Party cannot agree which part of the state should absorb losses from its property and credit bubble, so it keeps printing and exporting. Average people want the money printing that drives crypto higher, not the austerity that would stop it. The bill may eventually come due through revolution, but people seem to be opting out instead, through “lying flat” in China and quiet quitting in the West.

**Financialization doesn’t solve this.** Hayes sees Trump’s $1,000 accounts for newborns as neither good nor bad. They are printed money, so they help his holdings, but rents and other scarce goods will rise faster than the benefit. Trump, AOC, and Mamdani differ only in rhetoric; all print money for votes. Trump’s pandemic stimulus was the largest money-printing exercise since FDR, and Biden continued it.

He likes Zcash’s privacy narrative. He expects formal verification to address its minting issues around Q4, and notes that Maelstrom is an investor in Zcash wallet Zodl.

## FLOP: a currency for AI agents

Hayes came out of retirement to build **FLOP Network**, a payments network for AI agents. He expects agents to number in the quadrillions eventually, transacting with one another 24/7. Unlike humans, they have no dominant currency, and coordination requires something scarce: **compute**. FLOP applies Bitcoin’s design to GPUs. Miners provide useful inference for agents and earn block rewards and transaction fees in FLOP, tying the token directly to the resource agents need.

His thesis rests on **convenience yield**. People keep some money because they know they can buy food when needed; agents will similarly hold small FLOP balances because they can convert them directly into compute. With billions or more agents each holding a little, he believes FLOP **could become the largest cryptocurrency ever, bigger than Bitcoin**, whose economic base is a few hundred million humans.

His AI bear case also helps FLOP. When labs cannot afford their take-or-pay contracts, data centers will dump excess compute on the spot market. Cheaper compute will let users run more agents for more complex work; massive parallel compute is how labs are solving math problems now. The FLOP team consists of a CTO who worked on BitMEX’s trading engine, a CSO Hayes has known from banking for 20 years, Hayes himself, and many AI agents.
