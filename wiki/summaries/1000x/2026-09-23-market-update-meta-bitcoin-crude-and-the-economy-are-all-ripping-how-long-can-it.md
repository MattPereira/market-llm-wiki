# MARKET UPDATE: Meta, Bitcoin, Crude And The Economy Are All Ripping. How Long Can It Last?

**1000x** · 2026-09-23 · 1:01:26 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LbYfb1nZFl4)

## Diamonds, copper, and what makes a commodity trade

Lab-grown diamonds are crushing the scarcity on which natural-diamond prices depend. One host recounts meeting a lab producer that can make rough stones for about **$6 per carat** and is trying to turn that capacity toward diamond wafers for chips. Diamond conducts heat exceptionally well, so cheap, usable wafers could improve chip cooling; the host treats that as a promising possibility, not an established manufacturing breakthrough. They attribute the jewelry price collapse to new supply breaking a market long controlled by De Beers, though they disagree over whether buyers will accept lab-grown engagement stones.

**Demand over scarcity.** The diamond story becomes a trading rule: a commodity rising because one supplier restricts output is fragile when new production or technology arrives. They prefer commodities with broad, growing demand that producers still struggle to satisfy. Copper is their example. They say AI infrastructure and a strong economy are lifting demand, note a recent copper high, and expect the move to continue; one floats **$8 copper**. Their shale-oil example makes the same point in reverse: forecasts of scarce oil were upended when new supply appeared. They urge investors to identify durable demand trends rather than assume a supply constraint lasts.

## Higher yields, Bitcoin, and the AI trade

The hosts read a rising US 10-year yield differently from the usual higher-rates-hurt-risk-assets story. One says a yield above 5% reflects distrust of the Fed and an expectation of Treasury intervention and money creation. In that account, **higher yields can coexist with stronger Bitcoin, crypto, and gold** as buyers seek protection from currency debasement. He is less certain about the effect on all equities, but remains long Bitcoin, Intel, and the Nasdaq until the rally's fundamentals break. The other broadly agrees and expects the favorable market period to extend at least into early or mid-2027, while treating a later political change as a potential risk to asset prices.

They also argue that AI hardware and data-center spending may withstand inflation and rates better than banks and credit. One points to Meta's new AI assistant and an anecdote about an agent obtaining an airline refund as signs that assistants are moving from question answering into everyday tasks. He sees that use driving demand for chips and infrastructure, and regards Intel as a potentially better multiyear hold than Bitcoin, even though he finds Bitcoin easier to trade around bad news and support levels. These are their market judgments and anecdotes, rather than confirmed forecasts of adoption or returns.

**Crypto broadens.** They see Bitcoin's rebound alongside activity in Solana applications and a sharp Zcash rise as evidence that demand is returning beyond BTC. One host argues Zcash has acquired a monetary and privacy narrative, has access through regulated platforms, and could reach roughly **$5,000**; he favors moving some Bitcoin exposure into it while warning against leverage. The other is reluctant to chase a token after such a steep rise and says he missed the entry. The first host's Intel thesis is similarly ambitious: he expects a several-fold gain over three to five years, but presents that as his own position.

**Agents as crypto users.** The more cautious Zcash host is enthusiastic about AI agents paying one another on-chain. As assistants become familiar to mainstream users, he expects wallets and stablecoins to suit small machine-to-machine payments better than conventional bank accounts. He argues this could bring more activity and wealth on-chain than another round of institutional crypto products. This links their Meta-agent example to a new reason for crypto use, independent of a speculative token rally.

## Cheaper AI, expensive capital, and real estate

A chart of falling AI inference costs anchors their broader bullish case. One host says artificial thought is getting cheaper exceptionally fast, allowing AI to augment workers and raise output. The other expects lower prices to unlock more uses, comparing it to road expansion that induces more traffic. Together they expect greater AI use to support corporate profits and risk assets. They disagree somewhat on the bond path: one emphasizes weak bonds while equities absorb capital; the other thinks AI's eventual deflationary effect could bring yields down, even if fiscal concerns keep them high for now. He also argues that investors demanding higher returns from bonds can coexist with strong equities, especially when stocks offer a way to outrun dollar depreciation.

**Rates split property markets.** Real estate is the exception to their broad risk-asset optimism. They expect refinancing at much higher rates and building costs to pressure hotels, commercial property, and multifamily projects, while prime homes in concentrated wealthy areas may hold up. One host reports a hotel developer saying a new luxury property costs about **$2 million per room**; he expects some older hotels that counted on cheap refinancing to become distressed. He describes a sequence in which deal flow freezes, forced sellers reset prices, and new buyers return as liquidity recovers. He looks for that post-collapse recovery pattern in investments, citing crypto after FTX as an analogy. The other notes that wealthy travelers may still support luxury hotel prices even as the financing side weakens.

## A split economy and the argument over inequality

The hosts agree that spending is separating by wealth, but interpret it differently. Jonah worries that the middle is disappearing: luxury travel remains full and costly while ordinary experiences grow harder to afford, pensions and other fallbacks feel weaker, and younger people take leveraged or meme-coin risks to escape. He calls the result a **barbell America** and wonders whether AI will widen that gap enough to provoke political backlash. He still prefers investing in long demand trends over lottery-like trades, while acknowledging that getting a leveraged trade wrong can be devastating without a safety net.

Avi calls income inequality a “fake issue” in the sense that technology and capitalism can raise absolute living standards even as wealth gaps grow. He concedes that visible disparities produce real social instability, especially when social media makes comparisons constant. He argues that most people should save and own broad US equities rather than try to trade, and that AI gives motivated people tools to overcome a lack of capital or access. He also expects AI to increase inequality because those who use it most effectively will capture more value. Thus his claim concerns material progress, not a prediction that wealth will become more equal; Jonah remains concerned about the consequences of relative loss.

They end on how to respond to that comparison. Jonah urges listeners to learn from successful people instead of chasing their returns out of envy. Avi connects that to self-examination and looking forward: learn from mistakes, adopt useful tools, and take pride in work without making every activity a wealth-maximization exercise. Their shared advice is to pay attention to large technological shifts while keeping the pursuit of wealth from consuming everything else.
