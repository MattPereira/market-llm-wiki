# MARKET UPDATE: Alts Ripping, Economy Crushing, Scammers Scamming — What’s Next?

**1000x** · 2026-09-30 · 1:03:31 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-3I-oFY0aR0)

## Memecoin trust and a return to builders

Avi opens by defending his involvement in RuneScape Gold, ticker **GP**, against accusations that he took money from holders. He says he remained invested while others sold, and admits he failed to investigate the token's ownership distribution adequately despite repeatedly warning listeners against memecoins. He alleges that participants concealed substantial holdings in secondary wallets and promoted other tokens that drained GP's liquidity. These are his allegations and account of the dispute.

**Selling versus deception.** Avi distinguishes ordinary trading from promising never to sell while secretly liquidating undisclosed holdings. His objection is the mismatch between public representations and actual behavior. He argues that overwhelming people with accusations destroys trust and that influential participants must protect newcomers. Jonah agrees that deceptive promotion deserves condemnation, but emphasizes the opportunity cost: time spent in memecoin disputes could instead build trading strategies. His takeaway is to avoid the space.

Avi redirects attention toward products, revenues, and teams that can lower financial-system costs. After interviewing LayerZero's cofounder, he **bought ZRO and shorted ETH to isolate the relative trade**. He cites Robinhood's announced perpetuals offering through Bitstamp as another sign of integration. Their shared preference is for builders creating useful businesses over personalities extracting attention and liquidity.

## Hormuz: a bullish thesis with an escalation dispute

Jonah highlights **Javier Blas's column on Trump's advantage in the Strait of Hormuz**. As Jonah recounts it, Blas argues that oil's risk premium reflects fear that a cornered Iran must escalate. Jonah takes the opposite implication: he believes Iran is already using its available military leverage and cannot meaningfully intensify the conflict. He describes maritime defenses adapting and oil transit recovering from below 10 million barrels per day toward 13–14 million, versus more than 20 million before the war.

For Jonah, restored commerce and eventual de-escalation are strongly bullish for global risk assets. Avi challenges the certainty, pointing out that drones could still attack oil infrastructure. Jonah maintains that Iran is already trying its hardest and that defenses are improving. His conclusion depends on that military judgment; the discussion does not establish that further escalation is impossible.

## Which macro signals matter?

Avi calls much macro commentary “astrology for men”: entertaining narratives that rarely predict an asset's path reliably. He makes an exception for major regime changes, such as the inflation and rate shock of 2022. In his view, traders overreact to geopolitical stories while durable economic trends keep operating.

Jonah objects that macro trading is his profession and defines it as applying common sense over a longer horizon than short-term fears. He contrasts it with rigorous systematic trading, acknowledging that macro judgments are less precise. His examples are expecting an OPEC supply response after shale crushed oil prices and recognizing copper as a bottleneck in AI infrastructure. A coherent thesis need not forecast every data point to survive a temporary panic.

**Their agreement is about horizon.** Avi clarifies that he objects to investors treating every yield move or central-bank remark as decisive for Bitcoin or AI construction. Over five years, he expects stronger biotech businesses, copper demand, Bitcoin demand, and asset tokenization across a range of inflation and interest-rate outcomes. Jonah agrees that amateur attempts to trade short-term rate commentary put individuals against experienced institutions and faster machines.

## Megatrends and the individual investor's advantage

Avi defines a megatrend by first imagining the world in five or six years, then identifying assets that benefit. His examples include stronger AI capabilities, military investment, domestic chip production, and new nuclear facilities. Improving attitudes toward nuclear power and fewer regulatory barriers could, in his thesis, translate into higher uranium-miner profits. The work is choosing sound positions tied to that future and being correct about the underlying change.

Jonah's process is to identify a durable trend, then look for **bottlenecks, asymmetries, or temporary panic-driven mispricing**. He regards copper as a particularly attractive expression and cites Israeli property and the Tel Aviv stock market as examples where war fears created entries into longer growth trends. Those return and resilience claims are his assessments.

**Why the opportunity persists.** The hosts argue that institutional constraints can prevent apparently obvious trends from attracting enough capital. Portfolio managers face career risk, mandate restrictions, volatility limits, and drawdown rules. Avi uses a hypothetical copper allocation at a multi-manager fund to show why a manager might be forced out before a multiyear thesis pays off. Early Bitcoin presented a similar career problem.

An individual with continuing employment income can sometimes tolerate those fluctuations and add during dislocations. Avi distinguishes that investor from someone whose entire livelihood depends on trading, who may feel greater pressure to protect profits. Their proposed edge is therefore the ability to hold through volatility where professional capital cannot, rather than superior speed at reading news. Jonah credits successful venture investors with a related skill: placing a bet on a large trend and doing little for long periods.

## Technology, deflation, and the yield curve

Jonah sees easing oil disruption and technological progress as sources of deflation that should favor risk assets. Avi adds a possible bond-market expression: technology-led deflation could keep longer-term yields below shorter-term yields for an extended period. He says short rates are already rising faster than long rates and expects that relative pattern to continue over roughly five years.

Despite discussing the curve, Avi prefers expressing the thesis through technology equities, including the Nasdaq-100, and Bitcoin. Both see exponential technological progress as difficult for people to comprehend and still capable of supporting substantial further gains.

## Adding judgment to AI, and investing in its effects

Jonah calls people who simply forward AI output “meat proxies.” He argues that intermediaries lose their role if recipients can obtain the same answer directly. Investors and professionals should use AI while adding independent thought, creativity, and courage. He invokes Ben Affleck's view of AI as a tool for improving scenes rather than a substitute for an interesting screenplay.

Avi stresses that today's AI is the weakest it will ever be, so current shortcomings offer limited protection. He expects human connection to retain value in service work and engaging media. People may prefer a researched, opinionated human explanation to generated prose; he sees that preference as an investment argument for media. Jonah agrees on media but notes that humans also produce dull, unoriginal work.

**Healthcare as an expression.** Avi favors AI-enabled drug discovery through XBI, ARKG, and Eli Lilly, describing a goal of allocating around **20% of a portfolio for a fivefold gain over five years**. Jonah broadens the argument with a friend's healthcare-administration startup: automating faxes and appointments could reduce costs and raise provider profits. He sees benefits spreading throughout the economy, which leads him toward broad equity exposure.

## Broad indices or concentrated winners?

Avi increasingly prefers selected companies and sectors to broad indices. He argues that internet distribution, AI, and network effects accelerate consolidation: large firms can launch to vast customer bases, while more deployed robots generate more training data and improve their products. If gains accrue disproportionately to the largest winners, owning weaker index constituents creates a drag. He favors companies such as Google, Meta, and Apple alongside thematic sector exposure, while acknowledging that stock selection requires attention.

Jonah initially defends indices because they already own the dominant firms and replace failing constituents. Avi responds that the top ten companies capture much of the performance. Jonah becomes interested in a basket limited to the largest slice of the index, particularly for future contributions that would avoid realizing gains on existing holdings. They agree to investigate ways to obtain that exposure; they do not settle on a specific product.
