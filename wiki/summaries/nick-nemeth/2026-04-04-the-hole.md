# THE HOLE

**Nick Nemeth** · 2026-04-04 · [read](https://mispricedassets.substack.com/p/the-hole)

Private credit is the fuse; the bomb is the $10 trillion US life and annuity balance sheet, and Nemeth argues it is bigger than the Global Financial Crisis. US carriers hold **$1.54 trillion in affiliated reinsurance against $657 billion in total industry surplus**. Strip that credit out and 29 of the top 30 insurers are insolvent. The industry will compare these figures to total assets, a denominator that makes anything look small. Nemeth insists surplus is the only capital that stands between policyholders and insolvency, and concludes that a multiple of TARP likely does not exist at all.

## The man who saw inside

The piece rests on the work of **Thomas D. Gober**, a Certified Fraud Examiner with 41 years in the industry, including more than a decade on criminal insurance cases with the FBI. He flagged AIG's interlocking subsidiaries in 2009, presented to the Senate Banking Committee in 2022 and 2023, and filed an expert declaration in the Lockheed Martin v. Athene suit. Nemeth says no one, from Athene to the NAIC, has ever publicly challenged Gober's findings or answered his certified letters. For fifteen years Gober assumed captives were roughly 70% funded with real assets. Then Brookfield made a mistake.

## Three point seven percent

**The leak.** After buying American Equity for $4.3 billion in 2024, Brookfield had its tech team post the insurer's statements online, including those of three Vermont captives that had assumed about $7 billion in annuity obligations. Vermont keeps captive statements confidential and in 2024 made them immune to subpoena. Gober saved copies before they were pulled.

**The XOL asset.** The first captive held $128 million in real assets and a $1.48 billion write-in called "XOL Asset," an excess-of-loss contract with Hannover Life Reassurance. On the other side, Hannover's own Schedule S shows zero reserve and zero reinsurance payable, only an $11.6 million premium. Its filing, in other words, says it will never pay. Each captive's NAIC risk-transfer test came back negative, and the XOL fails all three asset criteria in SSAP No. 4.

**The totals.** Across all three captives, **$261 million in real assets backs $7 billion in liabilities: 3.7 cents on the dollar**. The phantom doubled in a year to $5.28 billion, against American Equity's roughly $3 billion surplus. Because American Equity owns the captives outright and guarantees them, Nemeth calls the arrangement circular rather than vertical, the kind of transaction people have been convicted for. Brookfield tried the same XOL on American National, a publicly filing insurer, and removed it within months after someone noticed.

**Every time anyone looked.** This was the third glimpse inside a captive. A would-be acquirer of Scottish Re found "almost no assets." PHL Variable's rehabilitator valued its XOL assets at zero, and a widow collected $300,000 on a $2 million policy. All three were nearly empty. The rest remain confidential.

## The industry's defenses

Nemeth rebuts three expected arguments. First, that XOL covers real tail risk: the captives' own tests show no risk transferred, and attachment points typically sit below the level where the insurer would already be in receivership. Second, that Vermont supervises captives: it has sealed their statements, and offshore domiciles, whose best rationale is taxes, answer to no US court. Third, that Schedule S discloses affiliated reinsurance: it shows Athene's $235.7 billion but not what backs it, and modco and retrocession chains are split across jurisdictions so that no examiner sees the whole.

## $1.54 trillion

**Across all 714 carriers.** Gober's tally from year-end 2025 statutory data puts affiliated reinsurance at 235% of industry surplus, ceded largely to entities in Vermont, Bermuda, Barbados, and the Caymans. Some has legitimate tax or capital purposes, but once a dollar moves to an affiliate, no one can verify what backs it. Nemeth judges the unbacked share worse than half. Even at 10%, $154 billion of phantom capital would push tens of billions onto the states.

**The biggest users.** Hannover carries $56.9 billion of affiliated reinsurance on $578 million in surplus, 99 to 1, and US policyholders have no claim on its German parent. Apollo/Athene totals $273.6 billion, Prudential $147.2 billion, and KKR's Global Atlantic $113.2 billion. Athene's $52 billion in affiliated investments is 26 times the NAIC guideline of 50% of surplus. Apollo tells regulators it is "on the hook" for losses while telling equity investors Athene is non-recourse. The $1.54 trillion also excludes cross-pollination, retrocession chains, and Europe.

## Three layers of obfuscation

Each time Gober exposed a scheme, Nemeth writes, the industry moved to a new one. **Layer 1** is the captive: about $600 billion in the US, with the gap plugged by XOL paper or contingent letters of credit. **Layer 2** is cross-pollination among troubled carriers. First Allmerica has $22 billion of affiliated reinsurance on $100 million of surplus. MetLife's roughly $10 billion recoverable from it exceeds MetLife's own $8.6 billion surplus. **Layer 3** is seven US reinsurers that funnel risk from about 670 carriers through 831 contracts. Of the $149.7 billion they owe cedents, $133.5 billion went to affiliated captives and offshore entities. Their combined surplus is $6.9 billion, so a 5.2% offshore shortfall makes all seven insolvent.

**The morphine.** Nemeth traces the pattern back to Gen Re's sham reinsurance with AIG, a case Gober worked. Its CFO, Elizabeth Monrad, called such deals "a little bit like morphine": hard to come off. The scheme ran through a Dublin subsidiary that "did not report to anyone"; Ireland regulates insurance but not reinsurance.

## How big the hole is

Nemeth gives three scenarios against $657 billion of surplus. If half the affiliated reinsurance is bad, in Gober's words, "it breaks everybody." If captives are funded at the 5% actually observed, they alone consume 87% of surplus. If only 30% is good, as Gober estimates, the hole is 1.64 times total surplus. Without affiliated credit, only MassMutual, a mutual with no private-equity owner, of the top 30 survives. Nemeth expects the industry to blame Brookfield. As far as anyone can see, though, Apollo, Golden Gate, and the other firms all do the same; Brookfield's staff just didn't know what to hide.

## How it starts

**The trigger.** Private credit is marked to model by managers who collect fees on it, which Nemeth calls worse than Enron's mark-to-market. The reckoning comes as a **downgrade**. Once a loan falls below investment grade, its NAIC designation and capital charge reset mechanically. At Athene's 69:1 leverage and 1.45% solvency margin, redesignating 5% of the portfolio and tripling its charge wipes out surplus in weeks. The insurer then needs its captive, which holds 3.7 cents on the dollar against a zero-reserved backstop. Annual statutory filings lag quarterly fund reports, so the system confirms losses late and gives no early warning.

**The Fed knows.** Fed researchers have found life insurers' risky debt exposure now exceeds their late-2007 subprime MBS holdings, with hidden leverage of up to 12:1. They put direct-loan recoveries at 33 cents on the dollar. Nothing has happened.

## The machine

The sham reinsurance and dividend extraction are the same event. When a company comes up short at year-end, the actuary is pressed to lower reserves. When that isn't enough, a captive deal cedes $1 billion of liabilities with half that in real assets. Surplus rises, the dividend is paid, and the stock rises. The next year's gap is larger. RBC ratios look clean because companies control what runs through the formula. Insurers own 17% of the US CLO market. AAA CLOs carry Treasury-level capital charges, and sponsor-originated private-credit CLOs, such as Athene's $6 billion from Apollo, have no independent bid. Athene's affiliated investments rose $12 billion in a year.

## What happens next

Guaranty funds cap most death benefits at $300,000 and annuities at $250,000. They are funded by assessments on surviving insurers, so if those insurers are also insolvent, there is no fund. Pension-risk-transfer retirees now hold claims on carriers like Athene, whose affiliated reinsurance is 57 times surplus. Nemeth calls the whole structure extraction of value from policyholders to shareholders. In past receiverships, day-one forensic work recovered money from the auditors and actuaries who signed off. A taxpayer bailout would remove the incentive to claw that money back, leaving the cost with policyholders and then taxpayers. **Private-credit downgrades have already started**, and Nemeth calls for a commission with subpoena power to expose the hole before they do. "This will blow up. The only variables are when and how much the public pays for it."
