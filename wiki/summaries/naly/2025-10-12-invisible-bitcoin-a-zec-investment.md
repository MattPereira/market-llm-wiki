# Invisible Bitcoin: A ZEC Investment Thesis

**Naly** · 2025-10-12 · [read](https://bloomresearch.substack.com/p/invisible-bitcoin-a-zec-investment)

Naly calls Zcash “invisible Bitcoin”: a scarce, self-custodial asset that can also hide transaction details. Bitcoin's public ledger proves its supply but exposes holders and lets observers trace coins. As Bitcoin becomes an institutional store of value, Naly expects demand for a second kind of digital money that preserves privacy and fungibility. The investment thesis joins that use case to Bitcoin-like issuance, improving shielded usability, and a simple story that investors can understand.

## Privacy as the founding idea

Naly traces the privacy problem to **Hal Finney**, who worried that Bitcoin's traceable transaction histories would distinguish one coin from another. Cypherpunk **Zooko Wilcox** and other cryptographers proposed Zerocoin as a Bitcoin privacy extension; after it was not adopted, they launched Zcash in 2016. For Naly, privacy means choosing what to disclose, rather than concealing wrongdoing. Zcash thus extends Bitcoin's monetary sovereignty into private transactions.

**The Ceremony.** Zcash's original shielded system needed cryptographic parameters whose compromise could have permitted undetectable inflation. A distributed setup tried to prevent anyone from retaining the secret material: participants used isolated computers and destroyed their outputs. Edward Snowden participated under a pseudonym. Naly points to the **[Radiolab account of the Ceremony](https://www.radiolab.org/podcast/ceremony)** because the origin story itself helps build belief and network effects around a privacy asset.

**Usable anonymity.** Zcash was an early live, permissionless use of zk-SNARKs, which let the network validate a transaction while hiding its sender, recipient, and amount. Users can hold transparent or shielded addresses and move between them. That choice creates an adoption problem: a thin shielded pool offers a smaller crowd in which to hide. Naly says fully shielded transfers were at times below 5% of activity, held back by wallet friction and proof-generation costs. New proving systems and the [Zashi wallet](https://x.com/zashi_app) have improved access; the article claims more than 70% of active wallets support shielded transactions and over 25% of circulating ZEC sits in the shielded pool. The thesis depends on usage growing alongside price, because cryptography alone does not guarantee practical anonymity.

## A path to broader adoption

Naly sees **Monero** as the closest monetary competitor. Its ring-signature privacy has acknowledged limits, while its always-private design has, in Naly's account, made exchange access harder. Zcash's transparent and shielded options, plus viewing keys that let a holder disclose selected records, may better accommodate auditors and institutions. Naly also contrasts Zcash's protocol-level privacy with **Tornado Cash**, whose sanctions and legal troubles illustrate regulatory risk for privacy tools. Both comparisons support a market-access argument, though Naly still treats Monero as an ally in preserving private money.

ZEC shares Bitcoin's 21 million coin cap and roughly four-year halvings, without an ICO, premine, or venture allocation. Naly argues it is **two halvings behind Bitcoin** in monetary maturity. Early miner emissions depressed its price, while the November 2024 halving reportedly reduced annual inflation from 12.5% to about 4.2%; the next would bring issuance toward 2%. In the article's October 2025 snapshot, roughly 16.3 million ZEC circulated and its $4.46 billion market cap was at a high even though its $272 unit price remained below earlier peaks. Naly interprets this gap, rising trading volume, and shielded activity as signs of accumulation rather than a failed price history.

**Institutional optionality.** Spot Bitcoin products have taught large allocators to value capped digital supply, Naly argues. A small secondary allocation to ZEC could give them similar scarcity plus privacy; the [Grayscale Zcash Trust](https://www.grayscale.com/funds/grayscale-zcash-trust) offers one access point. Naly also views ZEC's resilience during a broad crypto liquidation as evidence of positioning. These are arguments about prospective demand and the author's interpretation of market behavior, not established institutional flows.

## Why privacy may command a premium

Naly expects debt, inflation, digital identity, cashless payments, and surveillance to make private transactions more valuable. Bitcoin can hedge currency dilution, but public balances cannot shield holders from observation. Frozen reserves, debanking, and capital controls are offered as examples of why individuals, businesses, and institutions may want a discreet way to store and move value. Zcash's role is focused monetary privacy, rather than a broad application platform.

The author names material obstacles: privacy-coin delistings and possible tighter anti-money-laundering rules; difficult wallet and viewing-key workflows; occasional coordination problems between the Electric Coin Company and Zcash Foundation ahead of Crosslink; optional shielding that weakens the anonymity set; and competing zero-knowledge systems that attract developers and capital. Naly regards these as execution risks. Wider use requires a product simple enough for ordinary payments and enough regulatory access for people to obtain ZEC.

## The investment case

Naly's three pillars are **timing**, as halvings reduce sell pressure; **conviction**, as long-time holders remain after years of neglect; and **design**, as Zcash concentrates on private money. The article cites **[Frank Braun's ZEC analysis](https://frankbraun.org/zecbag/)** for a sizing exercise: an estimated $10 trillion in undeclared offshore wealth compared with a roughly $12.6 billion privacy-coin sector in 2025. If privacy assets captured 1%, 5%, or 10% of that offshore pool, Naly calculates implied ZEC prices near $6,000, above $30,000, and above $60,000 respectively. The author calls these scenarios, not forecasts; each assumes that demand reaches ZEC specifically.

In a portfolio, Naly sees ZEC both as a hedge against financial surveillance and as a high-upside bet on renewed demand for private, self-custodial money. The concluding claim is that even limited adoption by people who value privacy could matter greatly against ZEC's small starting valuation.
