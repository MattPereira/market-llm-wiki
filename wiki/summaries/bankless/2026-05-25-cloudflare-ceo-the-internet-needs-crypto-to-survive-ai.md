# Cloudflare CEO: The Internet Needs Crypto to Survive AI

**Bankless** · 2026-05-25 · 1:32:43 · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TKVY4hVAd9g)

Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince expects AI agents' web traffic to exceed human traffic in the first half of 2027. An agent researching a purchase may fetch a thousand times more pages than a person would. He thinks the internet can handle the traffic technically; the harder question is **who pays for the content and infrastructure** when agents retrieve answers without viewing ads or buying a subscription for each source.

## How answers break the web's bargain

Cloudflare began by putting firewalls in the cloud and giving small sites access to protection that otherwise favored large platforms. It built defenses against denial-of-service attacks and other tools as customers faced new threats. Prince sees that shared infrastructure as a way for independent websites to compete with walled gardens. After a long plateau in new sites and domains, he says easier creation tools have brought growth back toward early-2000s rates. Whether those sites can earn enough to survive is a separate problem.

Google's search and advertising systems made site visits convertible into revenue, funding broad access to information. But paying for traffic also rewarded cheap articles and incendiary headlines that drew clicks. Prince thinks that incentive has contributed to political division. He wants a replacement that rewards original knowledge rather than outrage. He cautions that the ad model did give people who could not afford subscriptions access to the same information as wealthier users; even tiny per-page fees could undo that benefit.

**The platform shift.** AI answer engines fetch source material and deliver a response directly, so users have less reason to visit the source and see its ads. Prince says publishers now receive referrals from Google at roughly one-twentieth the rate they once did; the corresponding rates from OpenAI and Anthropic are, in his account, roughly 1/1,500 and 1/60,000. Agents also do not click ads. Better answers help users, but if reporting and research cease to earn revenue, the fresh material those answers need will dry up. A single AI subscription that lets an agent read many publishers does not compensate those publishers individually.

Prince expects AI companies eventually to compete for distinctive material as YouTube came to compete for creators and Netflix for programming. He expects model capabilities to become easier for rivals to match, making exclusive knowledge a stronger reason to choose one service. A model with access to original medical research or local reporting could answer questions a rival cannot. He says AI companies he has spoken with are willing to pay creators, provided competitors pay under an industry-wide system too.

## Give creators control, then create a market

Prince says a market requires limited supply as well as demand. AI companies want more data, but crawlers can currently take much of it without paying. Cloudflare can help publishers restrict crawler access and negotiate from that position. He says Google's decades of crawling and licensing give it more pages than rival AI companies see: by his comparison, Google sees four pages for each page OpenAI sees and six for each page Anthropic sees. Those rivals may pay to close the gap if access cannot simply be taken for free.

Cloudflare's 2025 “Content Independence Day” gave site owners, including users of its free service, tools to control AI crawlers. It **did not block crawlers across every Cloudflare site**. Product documentation, for example, may be worth distributing freely because it helps customers use a service. An ad-funded publisher may instead need to withhold access until it is paid. Prince's rule is that creators choose according to their own business model.

That choice can frustrate a person whose agent cannot read a page. Prince's answer is that an agent acting for a human still bypasses the ads that pay the writer. He imagines an AI subscription passing some revenue to sources, as music streaming does, and perhaps telling researchers which questions have no good answers so they can create valuable new work. This could reward knowledge that fills gaps rather than another version of a widely reported story. Direct payment also makes the reader, rather than the advertiser, the creator's customer; Prince expects that to change what gets made.

**Two design risks.** A subscription-funded, trustworthy agent may be affordable mainly to richer users, leaving others with weaker, ad-funded services. A flat or auctioned price for content could also let the largest AI companies buy up access and exclude startups. Prince suggests contributions that scale with an AI company's revenue, though he offers no settled formula. His desired outcome is many competing AI companies, creators, and businesses rather than a few incumbents controlling the market.

## Payments at internet scale

Prince favors tiny payments when agents access content, potentially fractions of a cent per request. The web's HTTP 402 “Payment Required” response provides a starting point; Coinbase and others are developing payment infrastructure around it with Cloudflare. He describes the resulting x402 approach as **an evolving standard**, with both the rules and the payment rails still being worked out. Card fees would overwhelm such small charges, so he expects stablecoin rails to be necessary.

The scale is his main obstacle. Cloudflare handles about **500 million requests per second** and estimates that **1–10%**, or **5–50 million requests per second**, could be monetized. Prince says he has seen a blockchain claim capacity of about 2 million transactions per second, below even the low end of that range. He initially considered a stablecoin layer spanning multiple chains and would prefer to use another builder's solution, but says Cloudflare may have to help build one if none can scale. Although adoption could start gradually, Cloudflare's distribution across a large share of websites makes him unwilling to launch rails that might fail as usage rises. His numbers describe potential request volume, not payments already being made.

Cloudflare also serves many AI labs—Prince puts the share at about 80%—so he thinks it can help coordinate adoption on both sides. He does not expect one payment mechanism to cover every use: live retrieval, model training, licensing, subscriptions, and some residual ads may each need different arrangements. He likens the transition to music's move from piracy through paid downloads to streaming. The first payment system need only establish that source content has value; the durable business model can evolve afterward.

## Original reporting and the risk of concentration

Prince and his wife bought Park City's local paper, the *Park Record*, because local news mattered to them despite poor advertising economics. He says the paper could earn more from AI licensing than digital ads this year. An AI answering a question about a new Park City restaurant needs local reporting that cannot be reconstructed from another national paper. He sees the same value in original, specific research elsewhere; broadly duplicated stories are easier for AI companies to replace. He also attributes a rise in local election turnout from 23% before the purchase to 63% in a later off-cycle election to better coverage, an example of the civic value he wants the new model to sustain.

**The larger danger.** Prince worries that costly models and exclusive content could produce a few AI providers with their own newsrooms and political viewpoints, trapping subscribers in separate information bubbles. Agents choosing where people shop may also favor large firms that are easiest to transact with, eroding local businesses. He wants payment and access rules judged by whether they let new AI firms, creators, and small businesses enter and compete.

For a creator today, his advice is to decide who should access each piece of content, use tools to enforce that choice, and experiment with terms for paid access. He does not claim the final model is known. His firm positions are that creators need control over their work and that any workable compensation system must move very small payments at enormous scale.
