The reason the “Safety First” excuse for a cessation of AGI development by the three leading US AI labs is important for financial markets and global fiat liquidity is that these labs’ demand for compute backs over $1 trillion of investment grade and hundreds of billions of lower credit quality debt and loans.

**Captive Insurance**

*I want to thank Nick Nameth on Substack for this insurance scam education; please read his article “**[The Hole](https://mispricedassets.substack.com/p/the-hole?utm_source=publication-search)**”.*

The insurance industry is something I never studied. Therefore, the trend by large PE masters of the universe to fund their investments with captive insurers’ assets didn’t seem scammy to me. But as I dug into the mechanics, I discovered the location of the sucker.[2]

In every credit bubble, there is a sucker. Usually, it is a pool of retail investors managed by highly credentialed fiduciaries. The fiduciaries invest other people’s money and get paid twice: by charging management fees, and by selling investments to retail they own themselves, thus pumping their bags. Here, the suckers are buyers of life insurance and annuity products from US national insurers. To fully understand the scam, we must talk about why the golden era of PE ended, and what the PE bros did to keep the party going.

PE bros take an existing cash flow, apply debt, extract a dividend, and finally leave the company carcass to rot in the private market. When the public markets become frothy, they re-list this dogshit company and complete a circle of life. This is rational behavior when in the aftermath of the 2008 GFC, the private sector deleveraged, and the Fed dropped interest rates to zero.[3] Therefore, it made little sense to take risks and expand production to service broke ass plebes who were just trying to make monthly payments on a house with negative equity they couldn’t afford. PE investors, who could borrow at almost zero, looked for cash flow positive companies with low to no debt. They weren’t interested in producing a better product in higher quantities (their customers can’t increase consumption); they want to maintain cash flows where they are, cut costs, and load on debt. In this way, they can extract a cash dividend to pay their investors.

The law of diminishing returns hit PE fund returns hard at the same time the cost of capital started rising post-COVID. Because of cheap financing, winning a deal required paying a higher multiple for a stream of cash flows. As a result, PE fund returns fell. To raise the next fund, PE bros searched for a less discriminating pool of capital that didn’t want their money back. Enter the insurance company. An insurance company offers life and annuity products where policyholders pay premiums, and the insurer invests these sums to earn a return. The insurer will pay out at a later date, most times decades from when they initially sold the policy. This is the perfect pool of evergreen capital to invest in PE funds choked full of overpriced private companies and high yielding private credit. The PE bros purchased the insurance company, installed themselves as the investment manager, and then stuffed their dogshit down the throats of unwitting policyholders. This is called captive insurance.

The next leg of the trade, where it gets super scammy, is how the captive insurer funds the capital buffer the law requires it to hold against its assets. Financial assets sometimes fall, and thus insurance regulators require insurance companies to hold a certain amount of capital buffer against the assets for policy payouts. The insurance industry created reinsurers, or companies that exist primarily to take the risk of repayment off large insurance companies. Usually, insurers and reinsurers are not affiliated, and thus the reinsurer prices the risk at market. But that wouldn’t work for the PE insurance scam because the whole point was to find a sucker to buy assets without the PE fund putting material skin in the game. Therefore, the PE insurance company created a captive reinsurer directly affiliated with its parent. The captive insurer affiliate required little equity from the parent for its reinsurance policy.

These are all regulated entities, with periodic public disclosures. Would you purchase a policy from an insurance company you knew ran a scheme like this? To hide the scam, the American capitalistic system went to bat for the PE bros. In certain states, like Vermont, the law runs counter to ‌prudent national standards. The insurer and its affiliated reinsurer can create a reinsurance risk sharing asset, secretly, with as much or little capital buffer as they desire. The state regulator blesses the bastard and then places it under seal.

There is more, but before I continue explaining this brazen caper, let me contextualize this by creating a crypto hypothetical. Remember Terra Luna … Luna failed because USDT holders dumped the stablecoin and broke the dollar peg. What if Do Kwon had the juice of a septuagenarian New York PE bro, how would the situation have played out differently?

As the USDT price falls, Luna purchases an insurance company called Alameda Insurance; Luna will use the capital inflow to pump USDT in the market and hopefully restore the peg. Alameda sold life insurance policies to Californians and thus possesses a multi-billion dollar capital base. Alameda can’t buy a shitcoin stablecoin, but it can buy investment grade debt. Luna bribed a few Moody’s analysts so that they would bless its corporate debt as investment grade. To entice investors like Alameda, Luna paid 5% over the 10-year treasury yield in 2022. Wah out performance! Alameda incorporated a reinsurance company called Three Daggers in Vermont. Alameda pledged $1 of equity for every $100 of reinsured assets. Now Alameda told its regulators that the $10 billion of investment grade Luna debt it purchased with policyholder premiums was money good because if something went wrong Three Daggers would pay out. All good. But USDT continued dropping in price, the $LUNA shitcoin continued dumping, and a few weeks later Luna missed a coupon payment. Even though the Moody’s analysts got Daytona Rolexes as payment to lie about Luna’s creditworthiness, they couldn’t ignore a missed coupon and downgraded the debt to junk.

The downgrade triggers the downfall of this structure. Regulated insurers cannot avoid putting up more capital after downgrades. The problem is that the reinsurance asset was always bullshit. Three Daggers doesn’t have hundreds of millions of dollars of cash to send up to its parent so that the parent can satisfy the higher capital requirements. The jig is up; Alameda’s books now reflect its insolvency, and the regulators must clean up the mess.

What happens to policyholders? They get fucked. In most states, the maximum guaranteed payout is $250,000 to $300,000, but what if your policy should have paid out millions? Soz. Even more fucked up is that the surviving insurance companies pay for the guarantee. No firm paid into a guarantee fund before the credit event, which differs from how banking insurance works in the US. The FDIC requires banks to pay insurance premiums before an event.[4] Because surviving insurance firms pay into the guarantee fund after the fact, it encourages maximum risk taking by all parties because they never pay for their own bailout.
